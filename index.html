            What the smallest post-quantum signature costs

            Falcon next to the lattice signature it competes with and the classical ones both replace. Drawn to scale, in bytes:

            Falcon is hash-and-sign over an NTRU lattice: the secret key is a short basis (f, g, F, G with fG − gF = q, q = 12289), the public key is h = g·f⁻¹ mod q, and signing finds a lattice point close to the hashed message using the short basis. It cannot simply round to the nearest point (Babai's algorithm), because the rounding errors trace out the secret basis after a few thousand signatures, which is what broke NTRUSign in 2006. Instead it samples the step from a discrete Gaussian with floating-point arithmetic: small and precise, and fragile for the same reason. FIPS 206 is a draft; noble's module implements the round-3 submission and will change when the standard is final.

              What is known of FIPS 207, as of 2026-09-30

              The standard is not yet drafted. Everything on this tab follows the submitters' round 4 specification of 22 August 2025 and its reference implementation (v5.0.0). What NIST has said, cleaned up from Ridley's notes and checked where it could be:

              the name and the number

              FIPS 207, HQC-KEM

              • Settled in all but print. NIST's own presentation at the Sixth PQC Standardization Conference (September 2025) was titled “FIPS 207: HQC-KEM” (Angela Robinson, NIST). The name is HQC-KEM; it is not “ML-HQC”, and this page does not call it that.
              • No Initial Public Draft yet. As of about July 2026 NIST said the IPD was in preparation and expected shortly. CSRC was checked on 2026-09-30: no FIPS 207 draft is published, and the HQC news item is still the March 2025 selection (NIST IR 8545).
              • The private key may become the seed alone. The FIPS 207 team said it was inclined to make the seed the only permitted private-key format and to disallow caching expanded keys, deliberately differing from FIPS 203, which allows both. Here Alice's dk is the round 4 shape (ek ‖ seeddk ‖ σ ‖ seedKEM); every byte of it regrows from the 32-byte seedKEM, so a seed-only rule costs nothing but the regrowing.
              what may change, and what already differs

              Round 4 is not the standard

              • Hashes may move. NIST is considering changes during conversion, among them SHA3-512 in place of SHAKE256 for the seed expansion in key generation. An implementation built on the round 4 submission, this page's included, may not match the final standard byte for byte; the mathematics (the code, the weights, the decoder) is expected to stay.
              • Certificates. An individual Internet-Draft for HQC-KEM algorithm identifiers in X.509, draft-massimo-lamps-hqc-certificates, is reported with placeholder OIDs until FIPS 207 is published, not adopted by LAMPS. The datatracker returned nothing for it when checked on 2026-09-30, so treat that as unconfirmed.
              • What stays. Decoding a random quasi-cyclic code, the problem behind McEliece's system of 1978; a concatenated Reed–Muller and Reed–Solomon code; implicit rejection through a re-encryption check, as in FIPS 203.

              Construction (round 4, 2025-08-22): KeyGen(seedKEM): seedek, seeddk ← I(seedPKE); h ← XOF(seedek), a dense random vector; x, y of weight ω from XOF(seeddk); s = x + h·y; ek = seedek ‖ s. Encaps(ek): m of k bytes, salt of 16; K ‖ θ = G(H(ek) ‖ m ‖ salt); r₁, r₂ of weight ωr and e of weight ωe from XOF(θ); u = r₁ + h·r₂; v = C.Encode(m) + Truncate(s·r₂ + e); ct = u ‖ v ‖ salt. Decaps(dk, ct): m′ = C.Decode(v − u·y), then re-encrypt m′ with the same salt and compare with ct; equal gives K, unequal gives J(H(ek) ‖ σ ‖ ct), a random-looking key and no error (implicit rejection). The noise Alice's code corrects is x·r₂ − r₁·y + e, sparse times sparse; the tab counts its weight for real. The JavaScript is a port of pqlab.hqc, the author's Python implementation, which reproduces all 300 of the reference implementation's known-answer tests per set.

                1Seaography: vectors, lattices, and the nearest pointthe Red Queen's two maps

                “Would you tell me, please, which way I ought to go from here?”

                The idea

                A lattice is an orchard planted in perfect rows: a tree at every spot you can reach by whole paces along two paths. Two quite different pairs of paths can reach exactly the same trees. With a short, square pair it is easy to say which tree stands nearest to any spot. With a long, skewed pair you guess, and often guess wrong.

                What it is for. Finding the nearest tree is easy with the good map and hopeless, in hundreds of dimensions, with the bad one. ML-KEM and ML-DSA hand everyone a bad map and keep the good one secret.

                In Wonderland. The Red Queen's map is the bad one. It shows the same country; it just makes you run.

                In plain terms. A lattice is every whole-number combination of a few vectors. Many bases draw the same lattice; a short, square basis makes “which lattice point is nearest?” easy, a long skewed one makes it guesswork, and in hundreds of dimensions the guessing is the hard problem everything else rests on.

                The Turtle's statement, short

                L(B) = { B·x : x ∈ Zⁿ }              same lattice from U = B·T iff T is integer with det T = ±1
Babai's rounding:  v = B·⌈B⁻¹·t⌋       right with a good basis, often wrong with a bad one

                The Turtle's statement

                L(B) = { B·x : x ∈ Zⁿ }            a lattice is every integer combination of a basis
B and U give the same lattice iff U = B·T with T integer and det T = ±1
SVP: the shortest nonzero lattice point.   CVP: the lattice point nearest a target t.
Babai's rounding:  v = B·⌈B⁻¹·t⌋       exact with a short, nearly orthogonal basis; wrong with a long skewed one.

                Same country, different map. u₁ = 2b₁ + b₂ and u₂ = 3b₁ + 2b₂, so the two bases generate exactly the same points (the change of basis has determinant 1). With the good map, rounding the coordinates lands on the true nearest square every time. With the Red Queen's map, the parallelogram is long and thin, rounding is wrong more often than not, and the error is not small. Turning a bad basis into a good one is lattice reduction (LLL, BKZ); in two dimensions it is instant, in dimension 512 to 1024 (ML-KEM) or 1024 to 2048 (FN-DSA) it is the hard problem.

                Where it bites. Lessons 2, 4 and 10 are this picture with more dimensions. The dimension knob on a screen goes to 3; in FIPS 203 it goes to 1024.

                The Classics master's bench · Lagrange–Gauss reduction

                In two dimensions the bad map can always be mended. Keep the shorter vector first; subtract from the longer the whole number of copies of the shorter nearest its projection, m = ⌈⟨u₁, u₂⟩ / ⟨u₁, u₁⟩⌋; swap if the order changed; stop when m = 0. Each step is unimodular, so the lattice and its determinant never change. Lagrange (1773) and Gauss (1801) had it; LLL is its higher-dimensional heir, and BKZ, the heir's heir, is what the lattice estimates for ML-KEM and ML-DSA are about. In dimension 512 nobody knows how to finish.

                2Ambition and Distraction: arithmetic mod q, and Learning With Errorsthe Caterpillar answers with a small lie

                The Caterpillar answers every question with a small lie.

                The idea

                The Caterpillar is asked a sheet of sums and answers every one with a small lie. Without the lies, anyone could work the sums backwards and find his secret. With them, working backwards makes the lies grow until they swamp everything. Alice holds the secret, so she can take it away and is left with only a small wobble, which she rounds off.

                What it is for. This is Learning With Errors, the hard problem under ML-KEM and ML-DSA.

                In Wonderland. The Cat can read the sheet. The Cat cannot read the secret.

                In plain terms. Learning With Errors: publish t = A·s + e with a small error e. Without e, elimination recovers s at once; with it, the errors pile up. The key holder subtracts sᵀ·u and has only a small wobble left to round away.

                The Turtle's statement, short

                t = A·s + e  (mod q)                    public; s and e small
v − sᵀ·u = b·⌊q/2⌋ + eᵀ·r               round to 0 or ⌊q/2⌋; correct while |eᵀ·r| < q/4

                The Turtle's statement

                LWE (Regev):  public (A, t = A·s + e mod q), A uniform, e small.  Without e, Gaussian elimination finds s at once.
Encrypt a bit b:  r ∈ {0,1}ᵐ ;  u = Aᵀ·r ;  v = tᵀ·r + b·⌊q/2⌋   (mod q)
Decrypt:          v − sᵀ·u = b·⌊q/2⌋ + eᵀ·r  →  round to the nearer of 0 and ⌊q/2⌋.   Correct while |eᵀ·r| < q/4.

                Check your work · ML-KEM's numbers

                n = 256, q = 3329, module rank k = 2 / 3 / 4, error width η₁ = 3 / 2 / 2 and η₂ = 2, decryption failure probability 2⁻¹³⁹ / 2⁻¹⁶⁴ / 2⁻¹⁷⁴. The module version replaces each number in A with a polynomial of 256 coefficients (lesson 3), which is why k is 2 to 4 instead of 512 to 1024.

                The board's scheme, 500 times at every width E of the lies from 1 to 30: eight samples, q = 97, the bit placed at 48. Beyond it the lies add up like a random walk and failures climb. ML-KEM makes the same trade with centred binomial noise and a 256-coefficient ring, and FIPS 203 states the result it buys.

                3Uglification and Fainting in Coils: the ring, and the NTTthrough the looking glass, multiplication is pointwise

                “I never heard of Uglification,” Alice ventured to say. “What is it?”

                The idea

                Multiplying two long lists of numbers the schoolroom way takes every number times every other number. Step through the looking glass first, and multiplying is one number at a time; step back, and you have the same answer, far sooner.

                What it is for. Every real key in lessons 4 and 5 is a handful of these lists, 256 numbers each. The shortcut, called the NTT, is why they are quick.

                In Wonderland. Uglification, the Mock Turtle called it. It is only a change of view.

                In plain terms. The schemes work with polynomials of 256 coefficients modulo Xⁿ + 1, so a coefficient that runs off the end wraps round with its sign flipped. The NTT turns multiplication into one multiply per coefficient, which is why ML-KEM and ML-DSA are fast.

                R_q = Z_q[X] / (Xⁿ + 1)                Xⁿ ≡ −1

                4ML-KEM, the whole machine (FIPS 203)the Hatter re-seals the envelope himself

                “Open this, and there is room.”

                The idea

                Two strangers want the same secret key. One publishes an open padlock. The other locks a fresh secret in a box with it and sends the box; only the padlock's owner can open it. If anyone tampers with the box on the way, the owner gets nonsense back, never an error, so the tamperer learns nothing.

                What it is for. ML-KEM (FIPS 203) is how browsers and servers now agree the key that protects a connection.

                In Wonderland. The Hatter re-seals each envelope himself and compares. If it does not match, he smiles and pours you tea that is not tea.

                In plain terms. ML-KEM wraps an LWE encryption in the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform: the randomness is derived from the message, so the receiver can re-encrypt and check. A tampered ciphertext gets a pseudorandom key back, never an error message an attacker could learn from.

                The Turtle's statement, short

                KeyGen:  ek = (t̂, ρ),  dk = (ŝ, ek, H(ek), z)

                  The idea

                  The idea

                  11The Red Queen's race: security levels, Shor, and Groverall the running you can do

                  “It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.”

                  The idea

                  A large quantum computer would break today's padlocks, RSA and elliptic curves, outright, with Shor's algorithm. The new schemes are sized with that in mind, in five levels of strength.

                  Reading the Work column. Classical: 2^n is the cost of the best known attack, in units of one AES or hash evaluation; each bit doubles it. Symmetric: Grover's 2^64 for AES-128 is a count of steps that must run one after another, so AES-128 stays at level 1. PQC: no known quantum attack beats the classical one by more than the margins the levels already include, so its rows read the same with the box ticked or not.

                  Reading the Work column. These are security strengths in bits (SP 800-57 for RSA and curves, the AES and SHA references for the rest), not gate counts; the bench below converts the references to the Call for Proposals' gates (2^143 classical for AES-128) under MAXDEPTH. A classical key's strength only bounds attackers without Shor; Shor is polynomial, so lesson 13 prices it in logical qubits and Toffoli gates. Grover's 2^64 ignores depth limits, and P machines in parallel gain only √P. BHT's 2^85 for a SHA-256 collision assumes cheap quantum memory, which is why it is no cheaper in practice. The lattice levels rest on core-SVP sieving estimates with a small quantum sieving gain and do not charge for memory, so they lean conservative.

                  The Classics master's bench · NIST's categories in gates

                  The Call for Proposals (December 2016, §4.A.5) bounds a quantum attacker by MAXDEPTH, the longest serial run of logical gates it can manage: 2⁴⁰ (a year of the machines then envisaged), 2⁶⁴ (a decade of classical serial gates), at most 2⁹⁶ (a millennium at the speed of light). Grover must run serially, so splitting the search to fit under MAXDEPTH costs width: AES-128 key search then takes about 2¹⁷⁰ / MAXDEPTH quantum gates, against 2¹⁴³ classical gates. Collision search on SHA3-256 costs 2¹⁴⁶ classical gates. The five categories are these reference attacks, in this order.

                  “I went to the Classics master, though. He was an old crab, he was.” “He taught Laughing and Grief, they used to say.”

                  The idea

                  HQC hides a message by shouting it across a storm: about a third of the letters arrive wrong. Error-correcting codes, the kind that let a scratched disc still play, put the message back together. Only the holder of the secret can calm the storm enough for the codes to work.

                  What it is for. HQC is NIST's spare key-agreement scheme. It is built on codes instead of lattices, so one breakthrough cannot fell both.

                  In Wonderland. The Mouse's tale arrives through the pool of tears, a little soggy, and still readable.

                  Every lesson so far rests on lattices. HQC is the spare that does not: NIST chose it on 11 March 2025 as a second KEM built on a different problem, decoding a random code, the problem behind McEliece's system of 1978, the oldest public-key scheme still standing. The standard will be FIPS 207; no draft has been published yet, so this lesson follows the submitters' specification of 22 August 2025. The boards teach the mathematics, which NIST is not changing; names, key formats and hashes may change when the draft appears.

                  In plain terms. HQC hides a codeword under sparse noise in a quasi-cyclic code. The key holder cancels the dense part, leaving noise that flips about a third of the bits, and a concatenated Reed–Solomon and Reed–Muller code still corrects it.

                  The Turtle's statement, short

                  u = r₁ + h·r₂          v = C(m) + s·r₂ + e          s = x + h·y
v − u·y = C(m) + x·r₂ − r₁·y + e                 the h terms cancel; sparse × sparse stays sparse

                  The Turtle's statement · HQC-PKE

                  R = F₂[X]/(Xⁿ − 1), n a primitive prime.  No sizes over F₂, only the Hamming weight ω(v): secrets and errors are sparse, not small.
Hard problem (quasi-cyclic syndrome decoding): given dense h and s = x + h·y with ω(x) = ω(y) = ω, find x, y.
Keygen:  h ← XOF(seed_ek);  x, y of weight ω;  s = x + h·y;  ek = (seed_ek, s);  dk = seed_dk
Encrypt: r₁, r₂ of weight ω_r, e of weight ω_e;  u = r₁ + h·r₂;  v = C.Encode(m) + Truncate(s·r₂ + e, ℓ)
Decrypt: m = C.Decode(v − Truncate(u·y, ℓ))
Why it works:  v − u·y = C(m) + (x + h·y)·r₂ + e − (r₁ + h·r₂)·y = C(m) + x·r₂ − r₁·y + e     the h terms cancel
The noise e′ = x·r₂ − r₁·y + e is sparse × sparse, so sparse enough for C: it flips 34 % of the bits at HQC-1 (36 %, 37 % at HQC-3, HQC-5).
C = Reed–Solomon over GF(256) (outside) around Reed–Muller RM(1, 7) [128, 8, 64] with every bit said 3 or 5 times (inside).

                    13Jam to-morrow: Q-day, and harvest now, decrypt laterthe White Queen remembers the future

                    “The rule is, jam to-morrow and jam yesterday — but never jam to-day.” “It must come sometimes to ‘jam to-day,’” Alice objected.

                    The idea

                    Nobody has a quantum computer big enough to break today's padlocks, and nobody knows the year one will exist. The machines of 2026 are hundreds to thousands of times too small. But a secret sent today can be recorded today and opened the day such a machine arrives. So the question is not only “when?” but “how long must my secret stay secret, and how long will it take me to change locks?”

                    What it is for. Deciding what to move to post-quantum keys first, without guessing a date.

                    In Wonderland. The White Queen lives backwards and remembers the future: for her, the jam is always to-morrow, and the letters you post to-day are already opened.

                    In plain terms. A cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) needs about a thousand to a few thousand error-corrected logical qubits running a billion or so gates. The largest error-corrected demonstrations in 2026 hold about fifty. Nobody can date the crossing, so the planning rule is Mosca's: if the years your data must stay secret plus the years your migration takes exceed the years until a CRQC, data you send today is already exposed.
